# Relay 1 point

:::tip[Question]
I have a light switch in the pantry that turns on 3 recessed lights.
I want to add a relay so I can control this via zigbee.
I have 2 questions:

* In the switch itself (a normal light switch) there are only 1 blue cable on the "L" and another blue cable at the "switched L".
* The 3 lights may be in series. I don't know how are they wired. Do I put the relay on the first of the series? Meaning at the first of the three light points?
:::

:::info[Claude]
Good context to flag up front: what you're describing is very likely a standard **2-wire switch drop with no neutral** — not neutral wiring at the switch at all.

**On the two blue wires**

In EU/IEC-style cable, a simple 2-core cable is brown (line) + blue (neutral) by manufacture. But when it's used to wire a *light switch loop*, there's no neutral needed there at all — you only need permanent live in, and switched live going back out to the lamp(s). So installers commonly reuse the blue conductor as a second live conductor (the "switched live"), even though it's blue. Properly it should be sleeved in brown/red tape at both ends to mark it as live, but a lot of installs (especially older ones) skip that.

So: what you almost certainly have is **no neutral present in the switch box** — just permanent L in, and switched L going to the lights. This is the single most important thing for choosing your relay, because:
- Most Zigbee relay modules (2-relay boards, many Sonoff/Shelly models) need **L + N** to power their own radio/electronics.
- A "no-neutral" relay module exists specifically for this situation (e.g. Sonoff ZBMINI-L2, Shelly's no-neutral variants) — it sits inline with just L and switched-L, and draws its own power parasitically through the load. Works fine with LED downlights in most cases, occasionally causes faint glow/flicker on very low-wattage LED loads (a bleeder resistor at the fixture fixes that if it happens).

Given you've used Sonoff Zigbee relays before (the ZBRBS on your projector screen), if you go this route the equivalent no-neutral option would be the **ZBMINI-L2** rather than the standard ZBMINI which needs neutral.

**Where to physically put the relay**

Real mains lighting is basically never wired in true series (voltage would divide across the lamps, and one failure would take out all three) — even when it "looks" like a chain, it's almost always a **daisy-chain/loop-in** wiring, where each fixture just passes live and neutral through to the next one in parallel. So "series" here just means physically looped, not electrically in series.

Two options for relay placement:

1. **At the switch box itself**, if you go with a no-neutral relay — since that's exactly where L and switched-L already are, and no neutral is required there. This is the simplest option and keeps everything in one box.

2. **At the first light's junction box**, if you'd rather use a relay that needs neutral — because that's typically where a proper N will be present (the lighting circuit itself normally carries N through to the fixtures). In that setup, the switch's switched-L wire is repurposed as a simple momentary/toggle *input* to the relay's "S" terminal, and the relay itself switches L+N to the downstream loop feeding all three lights.

Either way — yes, if you use the light-junction-box approach, it should go at the point where the permanent L/N and the switch's wire *and* the wire continuing on to the other two lights are all present. In a loop-in setup that's usually the first fixture in the chain, since it's the one closest to (or fed from) the switch drop.

One caution: before opening anything, kill the breaker and verify with a voltage tester — don't assume based on color alone, since sleeving conventions get skipped often enough that "blue = live here" isn't something to trust blindly. If in doubt about which wire is actually the neutral vs switched-live at either box, a multimeter continuity check with the breaker off will settle it safely.
:::

> I'd rather go with a normal relay that requires a N, will the Sonoff Mini Extreme gen 5 work for this?
:::

:::info[Claude]
Careful here — "Sonoff MINI Extreme" is ambiguous, because Sonoff/resellers use "Extreme" for two different products:

- **ZBMINI-L2** ("ZBMINI Extreme," no-neutral) — Zigbee, does *not* need N, 6A max
- **ZBMINIR2** ("ZBMINI Extreme," Neutral Wire Required) — Zigbee, *does* need N, 10A max, explicitly listed by Sonoff's own docs as Neutral Wire Required

There's also a **MINIR4** which is the WiFi version (not Zigbee at all), so make sure you're not looking at that one.

Since you want the neutral-required Zigbee version, you want the **ZBMINIR2**. A couple of things worth knowing about it before you buy:

- It's designed to sit in the switch box behind your wall switch, with a detachable switch input — the switch input can be detached from the relay output, so the physical switch can act purely as a control input rather than a mechanical live cutoff.
- Handles up to a maximum load of 10A, plenty for 3 recessed LED lights.
- It also doubles as a repeater function, extending your Zigbee mesh — a nice side benefit for your network.

Given that this needs neutral, and you said you'd rather go the neutral route: that means installing it at the **light junction box** (as I described before) rather than the switch box, since that's where you'll actually have L+N together — not at the switch, where you only have the two lives. The switch's switched-L wire then just becomes the "S" input to the relay, wired at that junction box.

One more thing worth double-checking before ordering: confirm the exact SKU (ZBMINIR2) with the seller, since some listings and older reviews use "ZBMINI Extreme" loosely for the no-neutral L2 model too — the naming overlap is a common source of people ordering the wrong one.

It's a Sonoff Mini ZB1GS. It has N, L, L, Lout, Lout and S1
:::

:::info[Claude]
That model checks out — it's the Sonoff **MINI-ZB1GS ("MINI ONE Zigbee")**, part of the current Gen5 "MINI Extreme" line, and it does need neutral. So your terminal layout maps to:

- **N** — neutral
- **L, L** (two terminals, same connection) — permanent live in; the second one is just a convenience tap, e.g. to loop live onward to a neighboring device, not a separate input
- **Lout, Lout** (also two terminals, wired in parallel) — switched live out to the load. If you land a wire in each, both switch together simultaneously
- **S1** — input for your existing wall switch

That doubled Lout is actually handy for your wiring question: it gives you two independent switched-live outputs that always operate together, so if your 3 lights split into two branches at the junction box (rather than one single daisy chain), you can land each branch straight onto its own Lout terminal without needing a wire nut splice for that split.

For placement: since this relay needs N, and your switch box only showed the two blue L / switched-L wires (no neutral), this one has to go at the **light junction box**, not the switch — wherever you have permanent L, N, and access to the wiring for all 3 lights. There:
- Permanent L → relay's L
- N → relay's N
- Relay's Lout(s) → onward to the 3 lights
- The switch's switched-L wire (from the wall box) → S1

The physical wall switch then just acts as an input signal to the relay rather than actually cutting live power itself — the relay does the switching.

One heads-up since you're on Home Assistant: as of the info I'm seeing, ZHA support for the MINI-ZB1GS isn't officially merged yet — full functionality may need a manual quirk/script from Sonoff's help center rather than working out of the box. Worth checking that before you commit to this model if smooth ZHA pairing matters to you; Zigbee2MQTT support may differ, so I'd check current compatibility for whichever integration you're using before installing.

:::tip[Question]
> I don't know where that junction box is. I may use the rose of one of the three lights
:::

:::info[Claude]
Recessed downlights don't usually have a "rose" the way a pendant does — but they'll typically have a small connector/terminal block on a flying lead inside the fixture, and that's the equivalent spot. The key question isn't really "which light is physically first" — it's **which one (if any) has the switch's wire landing on it**, since only that point gives you everything the relay needs.

Here's the situation: somewhere, there's a point where your switch's two wires (permanent L and switched L) meet the lighting circuit's N and the feed to the lights. That point could be:

- **At one of the 3 downlights**, if it was wired "loop-in" style — most fixtures just pass L/N through to the next one, but one of them will have two *extra* wires beyond the standard loop, and those extra wires are the switch drop.
- **In a separate hidden junction box** in the ceiling void, if it was wired "junction box" style instead — common with downlight installs, where a dedicated box (sometimes called a connector/junction block) feeds each light with its own spur, and the switch drop also lands there rather than at any light.

So the practical way to find your spot:

1. **Kill the breaker** for that circuit and verify dead with a voltage tester before opening anything.
2. Open up each of the 3 downlights and look at the terminal block/connector. Standard loop wiring will show matching L/N/E pairs (in + out). If one fixture has an *extra* 2-core cable beyond that pattern, that's your switch drop — and that fixture is your relay location.
3. If none of the three show an extra cable, there's a hidden junction box elsewhere (often near the switch, or centered in the ceiling void) — you'd need to locate that instead, sometimes accessible via a loft hatch or by feeling around near one of the downlight cutouts.
4. Once you find the right point, confirm which wire is genuinely permanent L (still gets voltage regardless of switch position) versus switched L (only live when the wall switch is on) using your tester with the breaker back on briefly, switch toggled both ways — then kill power again before doing any actual wiring.

If it turns out the switch drop lands on one of the downlights, great — that's an easy install: L/N/Lout/S1 all right there in one fixture. If it's a separate hidden junction box, that's still the better spot to use even if it's more annoying to access, since splicing into the middle of the loop mid-run avoids leaving a messy tap on a fixture that wasn't designed for it.
:::

:::tip[Question]
What if I don't care about the physical switch and want to control it just with the relay?
:::

:::info[Claude]
That actually simplifies things a fair bit — you can drop the switch out of the equation entirely.

**Wiring changes:**
- **S1 stays unconnected.** The relay works purely from Zigbee commands; you don't need any external switch input at all.
- You still need the relay wherever N is available (same junction-box hunt as before) — that part doesn't change, since the relay itself always needs N to power its radio.
- What *does* change: you no longer need to hunt down which wire is "switched L" specifically — you just need a permanent L feed into the relay.

**What to do with the existing wall switch:**

This is the part that needs attention. Right now, power flows: permanent L → switch → switched L → lights. If you leave the switch mechanically in place and it's ever flipped off, no live will reach your relay's L terminal and the lights won't respond to Zigbee at all — so the physical switch would still have final veto power, which isn't what you want.

Two ways to handle it:
1. **Bridge the two wires together at the switch box** — join the permanent L and the switched-L wire directly (wire nut / connector, with the switch itself removed or left as a blank), so that wire is now permanently live no matter what. This is the cleanest option: the switch becomes irrelevant electrically, and you could put a blank plate over the box or leave the switch mechanism in place purely as a cover.
2. **Leave the switch physically installed and just always keep it toggled on**, never touching it again. Works, but it's fragile — anyone (including you, absent-mindedly) could flip it off and "break" the smart control until it's flipped back.

Option 1 is the more robust fix if you're comfortable opening the switch box and doing that splice — otherwise labeling the switch "leave ON — controlled by Home Assistant" and going with option 2 is a fine stopgap.

Either way, the relay installation itself gets simpler: find your N-equipped point (light fixture or hidden junction box), land permanent L → relay L, N → relay N, relay Lout → to the lights, and pair it in Home Assistant from there.
:::

:::tip[Question]
In this setup is it still important to identify the first light of the three?
:::

:::info[Claude]
Yes — for a different reason than before, but it matters more now, not less.

Here's the logic: with this setup, the relay needs to sit at the point where **permanent (unswitched) supply first enters the run**, so that everything downstream of it becomes controllable, and nothing stays permanently live outside its control.

Think of it as inserting a gate into the pipe. If your 3 lights are loop-wired (each one passing L/N on to the next), then:

- If you insert the relay **at the first fixture in that chain** (the one nearest the actual electrical source), its Lout then continues on to feed lights 2 and 3 — so all three end up downstream of the relay, and all three are controllable.
- If you insert it at fixture 2 or 3 instead, whichever fixture(s) sit *upstream* of the relay (between the real supply and the relay) keep getting permanent, unswitched power straight from the source — they'd stay on 24/7 regardless of what Zigbee says, since the relay never sits in their supply path.

So "first" here specifically means: the fixture (or junction point) closest to where the permanent L/N actually originates, not just any arbitrary one of the three. That's the one place where inserting the relay puts every downstream light under its control.

Same test as before applies for finding it: with the breaker off, check which fixture has the incoming supply cable arriving at it (versus just receiving a looped-through pair from a neighboring fixture) — that's your insertion point. If it turns out to be a separate hidden junction box rather than any of the three fixtures themselves, that box is where the relay goes instead, and all three lights then hang off its Lout.
:::

:::tip[Question]
So the risk is just if I instead of putting the relay in the first but in the last of the chain I'll be controlling one light and not three. But there's electric risk doing that? What if I misidentify the first light
:::

:::info[Claude]
Good question to separate out — and no, misidentifying which fixture is "first" isn't itself an electrical safety risk. The breaker/fuse still protects the circuit either way, and the only consequence of guessing wrong is functional: you'd end up with 2 lights stuck permanently on and only 1 controllable, not a fire/shock hazard.

Where real electrical risk *can* creep in during this kind of misidentification isn't the "which fixture" question — it's these:

**1. Mixing up L and N (not L position in the chain)**
If you land a wire in the relay's N terminal that's actually still a live conductor (or vice versa), the lamp holder can stay live even when the relay reports "off." That's a shock risk to whoever next opens that fixture expecting it to be dead. This risk exists regardless of which fixture you pick — it's about correctly identifying L vs N at whatever point you land on, so always verify with a meter rather than going by wire color or position alone.

**2. Cross-connecting wires within a busy junction**
If several cables land in one box (incoming supply, loop to next fixture, spare loop conductors, etc.), it's easy to accidentally join the relay's Lout back into a wire that's also fed from elsewhere — creating a short, or backfeeding voltage onto a wire someone would otherwise assume is dead. This is the scenario worth being careful about, more than "first vs last."

**3. Leftover unused/capped conductors**
If you end up not using a wire that used to carry the switched-L (because you're bypassing the switch entirely now), make sure it's properly capped/insulated and not left as a bare, energized stub.

**Practical mitigation, regardless of which fixture you pick:**
- Breaker off before opening anything, confirm dead with a tester.
- Before disconnecting anything, trace and label each wire's role (use continuity testing or briefly restoring power in a controlled way to identify live vs. loop wires) rather than assuming based on physical position.
- After wiring, before closing everything up, re-verify with the breaker on: relay's L terminal should show permanent voltage, N should test as neutral, and Lout should only show voltage when the relay is switched on via Zigbee. If that all checks out, you've confirmed correct topology regardless of whether you happened to pick the true "first" fixture.

So: worst case of picking wrong is "only 1 of 3 lights is smart," which you'd notice immediately and just move the relay to a different fixture. The things that actually matter for safety are identifying L/N correctly and avoiding cross-wiring in a crowded box — both of which you should verify with a meter rather than assumption either way.
:::
